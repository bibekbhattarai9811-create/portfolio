Overview

The problem

Making one Why It Broke episode used to take hours: writing the script in a chat window, recording voice files, finding images, then placing everything by hand in a video editor.

Most of that time was mechanical work that software is good at. So VideoForge first became an editor, then an autopilot that now makes a Short every day.

Approach

The autopilot

  1. Research and script

    Claude researches the topic with web search and writes a fact-checked script with its sources. A second pass polishes it against the facts and rejects big rewrites.

  2. Visuals

    It pulls relevant, properly licensed photos from Wikimedia Commons and draws its own animated diagrams for stats, timelines and comparisons.

  3. My voice

    Long videos are narrated in my own voice. I record once, and a local voice clone (Chatterbox, running on my laptop's CPU) reads new scripts; my own recordings are aligned word by word with Whisper.

  4. Music and edit

    It composes an original background track in code, so there's no copyright risk, then edits everything to the narration with captions and a 1080p export.

  5. Package

    Every video gets a title, a description with sources and photo credits, tags, three thumbnail options to A/B test, and three auto-picked clips cut into extra Shorts.

Decisions

Key decisions

My voice, not a stock AI voice

Long videos use my own voice, to stay clear of YouTube's policy on fully automated content and to sound like a real channel.

A custom timeline, about 7x faster

MoviePy's built-in compositing was too slow for long videos, so the editor renders through a single custom clip that decides frame by frame which shot is visible.

Ad-friendly by design

The true-crime channel only covers solved, convicted cases, with no gore and careful wording, so episodes stay advertiser-friendly.

I stay the editor

Finished videos land in my AI Office inbox and an upload folder for review; nothing is published without me.
